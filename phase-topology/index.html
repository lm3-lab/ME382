The model

Two phases — a liquid and a single solid solution — both regular, with the solid referenced to zero at each pure end so the melting terms carry all the temperature dependence.

G_l(x) = (1-x)·ΔH_A·(1 - T/Tm_A) + x·ΔH_B·(1 - T/Tm_B) + Ω_l·x(1-x) + RT·s(x) G_s(x) = Ω_s·x(1-x) + RT·s(x) s(x) = x·ln x + (1-x)·ln(1-x)

Equilibrium at each temperature is the lower convex hull of the two sampled curves, pooled. A straight segment of that hull is a tie-line. An invariant is a temperature at which one segment carries three contacts at once — the moment a liquid and two solids share a common tangent.

The whole distinction, in one line

At the invariant, call the two solid contacts xα < xβ and the liquid contact xL.

x_α < x_L < x_β → EUTECTIC L → α + β x_L outside that → PERITECTIC L + β → α

Same convex-hull event, same code path, different contact ordering. If the liquid sits between the two solids it decomposes into both on cooling. If it sits outside them, it must instead consume one solid to make the other. That is the entire difference, and the readout above is computed from exactly this test.

What actually drives a peritectic

Correction to the usual claim

It is often said that a large difference in melting points turns a eutectic into a peritectic. That is wrong as stated. Hold the melting enthalpies equal at 12 kJ/mol and push TmB from 900 K all the way to 2400 K, and the system returns a eutectic in essentially every cell — only two isolated peritectic cells survive, at TmB ≥ 1800 K and right at gap onset (Ωs = 12), and they are gone by Ωs = 14. Open the melting parameters above and run it.

What matters is asymmetry in the melting entropy, ΔSm = ΔHm / Tm:

A 12000 / 700 = 17.1 J/mol/K B (branch 2) 40000 / 1400 = 28.6 J/mol/K

A large ΔSmB makes the B-side liquidus steep, which confines the liquid to the A-rich corner. Meanwhile the solid miscibility gap is symmetric in this model — it is always centred on x = 0.5. So when the widening gap finally reaches the solidus, both solid contacts lie to the B-rich side of the liquid contact, and the reaction is necessarily peritectic. Raising Ωs widens the gap until xα slides left past xL, and at that crossing the peritectic becomes a eutectic. The two facts have to be read together: a steep liquidus puts the liquid off to one side, and a centred gap keeps the solids where they are.

Also tested, also not the answer

A subregular skew on the solid, Ωs(x) = Ω0 + Ω1(1-2x) with Ω1 up to 24 kJ/mol, shifts xL and xα together and never flips their order. Skewing the gap is not a route to a peritectic either.

Notes on the construction

Composition is sampled on a uniform grid of N = 401 points. Both phases' points are pooled as (x, G, phase) and passed through a monotone-chain lower hull that carries the phase tag along. A hull edge counts as a tie-line when it spans more than 1.5 grid steps.

The invariant is found by cooling until the first solid–solid tie-line appears on the hull. The liquid contact is then read one step above that temperature, never at it: at the invariant itself the liquid contact is on the point of vanishing and is numerically degenerate. This detail is easy to get wrong in a way that silently reports a lens — a solid miscibility gap that opens below the solidus produces a solid–solid tie-line with no liquid contact anywhere above it, and that is a lens, not an invariant.

Regular-solution model, ideal liquid, R = 8.314 J/mol/K. Built to sit alongside the molecular dynamics movie; the thermodynamics here is the equilibrium end of what the MD shows kinetically.
