The free energy as a function of composition

We decide to make a binary AB alloy with a composition 0 <= x <= 1.

There can be a mixture of separated phases: we can work out how much of each, and their composition, with the lever rule.

Whether phase separation happens or not depends on the interplay of entropy (number of distinct configurations) against energy (bonding energy per configuration)

When the energy doesn't depend on composition, entropy dominates, but many mixtures prefer to order (e.g. lower energy by maximising AA BB pairs over AB pairs).

This is captured in the free energy at a temperature T: F(x)=E(x)-TS(x), where S is the entropy. Higher entropy, lower free energy.

1 · Entropy: freedom of arrangement

x = 0.01. One B atom among A. Move it anywhere and the picture barely changes — few distinct arrangements.

x = 0.50. Half and half. Astronomically many distinct arrangements.

More entropy = lower free energy Having more ways to arrange the atoms raises the entropy and lowers the free energy, at most in the middle for the binary alloy. On its own this puts a single minimum at x=0.5.

2 · Energy: does it cost to mix species?

We have considered the bonding curve for single element systems. For binaries, what matters is how an A–B bond compares with the average of an A–A and a B–B, marked here as a dashed line.

Mixing. The A–B well sits on the average, so swapping who sits next to whom costs nothing. The bonding contribution to the curve is flat.

Ordering. The A–B well is shallower than the average, so every unlike neighbour costs a little. A half-and-half mixture has the most of them, so the contribution humps up in the middle.

3 · Put them together

The result. With no bonding penalty, one minimum in the middle — roughly what the liquid does. Add the hump and it splits into two, one A-rich and one B-rich. That double well is what you are about to make with the slider.

What you are looking at

The left panel shows how much energy the liquid and the solid cost at each composition, at one temperature. The alloy always takes the cheapest option available to it — and a mixture of two phases is an option, costing whatever lies on the straight line joining them.

So imagine running a string underneath both curves and pulling it taut. That line is the convex hull, drawn dashed in the panel. Where it hugs a curve, you get that single phase. Where it leaves the curves and runs straight, the alloy separates into the two phases sitting at the ends of that straight stretch. The right panel is nothing more than this answer worked out at every temperature and stacked up.
